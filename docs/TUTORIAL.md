# Learn the slide rule

Choose the subtly tinted **Learn the slide rule** button in the header. The course has **89 lessons in
13 sections**, covering 50 principal scale IDs. Begin with **The basics**, or use
**Section** and **Lesson** to choose a technique. Choose **Watch demonstration**.

The persistent **Problem** states the calculation in mathematical symbols, with
its given quantities and units. It changes to a separate question when you choose
**Try it yourself**. The problem remains visible during demonstrations, replay
and practice; the narration explains each physical operation below it.

The actual slide and cursor move on a simplified teaching instrument, with the
scale’s formula caption visible beside its name. Captions refer to each scale’s
local input; the narration explains factors of ten, branches and folds. Each
movement changes one part and stops for inspection. **Next movement** continues;
**Replay movement**, **Pause movement** and **Resume movement** control playback.
The moving part is highlighted. Inspect the graduations and the digital readings
below the rule together. Tall layouts remain in ordinary document flow: scroll
vertically as needed and replay a movement once its relevant rows are in view.

## Read the scales with their stated conventions

Equal ratios span equal distances on C and D. Sliding one scale shifts those
distances, so an alignment represents a constant multiplier. The course applies
this idea to products, ratios, conversion and multiplication of logarithms.

C/D, CI/DI and folded scales supply significant figures. Estimate the decimal
place from the problem. A, B and K cover several decades. Dedicated R1/R2 and
Q1/Q2/Q3 branches require grouping the radicand exponent in twos or threes. The
same cursor coordinate can represent different requested roots when the selected
branch or external power of ten changes. Their printed labels are √₁/√₂ and
∛₁/∛₂/∛₃. The inventory explains the general grouping rule, including inputs below one.

CF and DF are folded at π, with their unit indexes inside the graduated length.
CIF/DIF read reciprocal mantissas. At regular positions away from the exact 10|1
break, DF × DIF = 10. At the exact break the emulator normalises both readings to
1; the lessons explicitly identify the physical index and required decimal range.
CI moves with the slide; DI stays fixed. Their coincidence at zero offset does
not make their behaviour interchangeable after moving the slide.

An LL reading is the full numerical answer: retain its actual decimal places.
The generic LL0–LL4 rows use positive exponents D/1000, D/100, D/10, D and 10D;
LL00–LL04 use the corresponding negative exponents. Changing C/D index can require
another LL bank. Manufacturer LL numbering varies. Near-one readings preserve
additional decimal places for their excess above one; very small inverse values
remain nonzero. L gives a base-ten logarithm mantissa, while the LL lessons use
natural logarithms.

L and the linear ln row also support numbers outside 1–10: keep the decade
separately. For x = m × 10ⁿ, log₁₀x = n + log₁₀m and ln x = n ln(10) + ln m.
The linear ln row (also labelled Ln on some instruments) is different from the
Flying Fish Ln banks, which display exponentials. CF/M and DF/M fold at
ln(10) = 1/M, where M = log₁₀(e). They make common/natural logarithm conversion
convenient and can avoid an off-scale product. π-folded CF/DF remain convenient
for circle calculations; neither fold is universally preferable.

The Faber-Castell 2/83N W₁/W₂ pair covers one decade in two full-length halves,
with W₁′/W₂′ moving together. Arithmetic needs the W index and branch specified
in each instruction. Their effective length is twice C/D’s; the emulator does
not promise a particular physical reading precision. The actual √10 value is
shown rather than claiming the original coloured index engraving.

Thornton Ps gives the Pythagorean complement. The Pt lessons use the emulator’s
H₁/H₂ result scales to illustrate √(1 + t²); these differ in input direction
from the historical Pt scale. Sd/Td/ISd/ITd are **normalised layout studies, not
calibrated historical correction scales**. Their labels locate angles or
function inputs. The lessons compare them with calibrated S/T₁ readings and
explain their intended mathematical roles; D is not a correction-factor readout.

“Neat tricks” combines ordinary scale operations with explicit mental arithmetic.
The slide rule supplies products, quotients, roots and function values; the
narration identifies each addition, subtraction or decimal adjustment.

Angles are in degrees. S and T₁ use D/10; T₂ reads the tangent directly from D.
The emulator’s generic ST is sine-calibrated: D/100 gives the sine, while its
use for tangent is an explicitly quantified small-angle approximation. P gives
√(1 − t²) with t = D/10. H₁ uses t = D/10 and H₂ uses t = D in √(1 + t²).

Hyperbolic arguments are dimensionless. Sh₁ uses D/10 for sinh, Sh₂ and Ch use D
directly for sinh and cosh, and Th uses D/10 for tanh. Th ends at argument 3,
before the right D endpoint; tanh of a finite argument never equals one. Inverse
cosh exercises specify the nonnegative solution. In the catenary model
`y = a cosh(x/a)`, the lowest point has `y = a`; the demonstrated y is the stated
vertical coordinate. Its slope `dy/dx = sinh(x/a)` is dimensionless.

Geometry problems define radius, diameter, semi-axes, perpendicular height and
angle as appropriate. Areas use squared length units and volumes cubed units.
Any subtraction, percentage conversion or external power-of-ten adjustment is
identified explicitly; the logarithmic rule does not directly add or subtract.

## Practise the movements

At the end of a demonstration, choose **Try it yourself**, or continue to another
lesson or section. New techniques have separate practice problems. Drag the
moving scale row to move the slide; drag the red hairline to move the cursor.
The Slide and Cursor grips, their arrow buttons and focused-grip arrow keys also
work. Usual fine-positioning shortcuts remain available. Moving the instrument
during a demonstration stops playback; **Replay movement** starts that step again.

Each instruction asks for an alignment. **Check alignment** reports which
constrained parts need adjustment. After success, **Next practice step** continues.
Moving a constrained part invalidates its current check. The final successful
check records practice and offers continuation or **Finish tutorial**.

The check accepts a small physical positioning tolerance. It does not grade
which row you mentally read, decimal placement, units, a numerical answer or
external arithmetic. In particular, several LL/root rows can share a hairline
coordinate while giving very different answers. Use the persistent problem and
the named row; read the successful feedback as an alignment confirmation.

On narrow screens the view follows demonstrated positions horizontally. Grip
and keyboard practice also bring the hairline into view. Direct instrument
pointer dragging keeps the view still until release to avoid changing coordinates
beneath the pointer. Sideways scrolling remains available. With reduced motion,
each requested movement changes directly to its endpoint. Playback pauses when
the browser loses focus or the page becomes hidden.

## Return to your work

**Close tutorial** and **Finish tutorial** restore the original applied layout,
positions, magnification, motion step and loupe setting. The unapplied JSON draft
and scale-edit Undo history survive changes between teaching layouts. Layout
editing is temporarily hidden and locked. A pending JSON file import is cancelled
at lesson entry; import it again afterwards if needed.

Training positions and course progress are not saved as your programme.
**Save standalone copy** saves your original programme, with the course closed
and fresh progress when opened. Page-theme choices continue to behave normally.

## Extend and verify the course

`src/tutorial/course.js` assembles thematic sections; subject modules under
`src/tutorial/lessons/` hold their data. `lesson-data.js` supplies small data
constructors, `lessons.js` exposes the flat list and alignment policy, and
`programme.js` owns 35 named teaching profiles. Animation and original-state
ownership remain in `animation.js` and `workspace.js`; the UI and application
render the actual instrument and protect storage/exports.

Every lesson and practice needs a separate unanswered `problem` string. Every
demonstration step names its physical target, highlighted part and narrated scales.
Only one component changes per movement. Practice names all coordinates needed
for that particular alignment; a fixed-scale reading can ignore the slide.
Tolerance is approximately three screen pixels, bounded between 0.0015 and 0.004
of the graduated length. This is an alignment tolerance, not a uniform numerical
accuracy guarantee on steep nonlinear scales.

Add independent expected-answer fixtures for every demonstrated and practised
reading. The course-wide test requires each lesson to belong to exactly one
reviewed oracle; tiny values need relative-error checks and near-one functions
need checks of their excess above one. Keep the inventory and coverage contract
current when adding a scale or changing a question.

```sh
node --test tests/unit/tutorial-*.test.js
npm run verify
npm run test:tutorial
```

Unit fixtures verify mathematics, scheduling and state boundaries. Actual fonts,
pointer interaction, screen layout, focus and assistive technologies require
native browser review. The current handover records executed checks and limits.
